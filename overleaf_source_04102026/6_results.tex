\autoref{tab:main_results} places the answer measures beside the derivation measures; we read the
final answer first and then what the derivation shows that the answer cannot.

\paragraph{A Ceiling and a Graded Order.} The final answer barely ranks the strongest models: the
first five are near the ceiling of the evaluation set, scoring 0.94 to 0.98 even on Advanced
templates, and only three of their ten pairs differ after Holm's correction.
The models differ in reliability more than in reach: no model fails every instance of more than
five of the 150 templates, yet the share of templates solved on every instance runs from 31\% to
90\%, a difference that one instance per problem would hide.
Across the eleven models the order is graded rather than tiered: 36 of the 55 pairs differ, and
only \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} differs from every other model (\autoref{tab:matched}).
The three models run with reasoning at medium effort all gain, \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini} by 0.118 and
into the first five; at their providers' defaults, the order splits into two tiers.

\paragraph{Derivations Behind Correct Answers.} The derivation shows what the final answer cannot:
how closely a model follows the gold route, and the slips a correct answer passes.
Neither the final answer nor MC as scored separates \texttt{Claude Sonnet 5} from
\texttt{DeepSeek V4.1 Flash}; matching alone does, but not once the milestones the judge rules
unnecessary for each model's route are set aside (\autoref{tab:coverage_variants}).
What separates them is how closely each follows the gold route, so MC ranks how much of that route a
model shows, not how soundly it reasons.
Correct answers can still contain a wrong calculation: the arithmetic check flags a displayed
calculation that does not follow from its own operands in up to 23.2\% of a model's correct
answers, and of the 189 flags a domain expert decided on, 171 are real slips (precision 0.905;
\autoref{tab:coverage}).
These flags miss most flawed steps: in the expert study, the arithmetic and judged step checks
together find only 0.36 of those the domain experts marked inside correct answers.

\paragraph{Derivations Behind Wrong Answers.} A wrong final answer hides how much of the derivation
is right: the last four models' wrong answers still state 46\% to 59\% of the gold milestones,
against a chance floor of at most 17.5\% (\autoref{tab:coverage}), and up to 25\% of them reach
every milestone yet end in an incorrect answer.
Where a derivation breaks, the judge's rulings name the gold quantity it fails to reach: a milestone
ruled missing marks most of these models' wrong answers (63\% to 78\%) but only 20\% of
\texttt{Claude Sonnet 5}'s, whose wrong answers mostly reach every milestone and still miss the
answer.
Weaker models more often lose answers on derivations they can carry out: on the 58 templates whose
instances all follow one derivation, the first five solve some but not all instances of 7\% to 34\%
of the templates and the other six of 14\% to 59\% (\autoref{appendix:results}), consistent with
the expert reading, in which calculation errors dominate (\autoref{subsec:error_analysis}).

\paragraph{Stability of the Order.} Every pair of models that differs holds under the small changes
we test: rewording, a looser or stricter answer tolerance, and repeated decoding reverse none of
them; only the reasoning setting, or how empty responses count, moves a model past one it differs
from.
Under an expert-checked paraphrase that keeps every number, unit, and symbol, no model's FAC
changes significantly, and for nine of the eleven models the change is bounded within $\pm 0.05$
at 90\% confidence; halving or doubling the tolerance swaps no pair of models that differs, and
decoding the four lowest models three more times moves FAC by a standard deviation of at most
0.008 (\autoref{appendix:paraphrase}).
Counting only the responses that state an answer moves \texttt{GLM-5.3}, whose 99 responses are
empty at the output ceiling, from seventh to first, so its place in~\autoref{tab:main_results}
reflects the ceiling.
The paraphrase bounds memorized wording only, since the numbers and the problem structure stay.

\subsubsection{Performance by Engineering Branch and Domain}
\label{subsec:branch_domain}

No branch is hard for every model: the lowest branch differs from model to model, and only one of
the 110 within-model branch comparisons holds after correction (\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, electrical
above civil); with 30 templates per branch, differences below 3 to 19 points cannot be told from
sampling noise.
Weaker models are less even: the first five stay within 0.018 to 0.053 of their best branch and
above 0.88 in every domain, while the last five spread 0.071 to 0.226 and each falls below 0.81 in
some domain, though these spreads carry no test.
Thermodynamics is the lowest domain for four of the eleven models, but the domain means carry no
test, so this ranks domains, not fields (\autoref{appendix:branch_domain}).

Harder problems cost the weaker models most.
Every model scores lower on Advanced than on Easy templates (\autoref{fig:level_bars}), by 0.006 to
0.181, but the gap holds after Holm's correction only for \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} under Welch's
$t$-test, the test we read because scores vary more across Advanced templates, and for four models
under the permutation of level labels (\autoref{tab:level_gap}).
The length of the derivation splits the models more cleanly: on instances with six or more gold
milestones, the share of responses scored 0 reaches 0.104 to 0.254 for the last five against at
most 0.061 for the first five, and over readable responses each further milestone raises the odds
of a wrong answer by about a fifth when pooled over the models (odds ratio 1.21, 95\% interval 1.09
to 1.33), a slope that holds per model for three of the eleven (\autoref{tab:level_gap}).

\subsubsection{Reasoning, Equations, and Tools}
\label{subsec:reasoning_equations_tools}

Three experiments on a 450-instance subset test whether a larger model, the governing equations, or
a Python interpreter changes these results, and a fourth adds reasoning at medium effort
(\autoref{appendix:experiments}); each change starts from the providers' defaults and is paired with
the default responses on the same instances.

\paragraph{Flagship Anchors and Reasoning.} A larger model does not clearly raise the ceiling, but
reasoning helps a flagship as it helps a small model: of the two flagships run on the subset as
anchors, \texttt{GPT-5.4} and \texttt{DeepSeek V4 Pro}, the best run, \texttt{GPT-5.4} with
reasoning at medium effort (0.997), still lies inside the interval of \texttt{DeepSeek V4.1 Flash}.
\texttt{GPT-5.4}, which returns no reasoning tokens at its default, gains 0.046 with reasoning and
sheds most of its arithmetic flags, and \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini} with the same setting gains 0.057 in
MC while its flags on correct answers roughly halve: reasoning changes the derivation, not only the
answer.

\paragraph{Open Book.} Knowing the formulas limits only the weakest model: given each template's
governing equations, without data tables, \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} answers more correctly ($+0.068$),
partly because fewer of its responses are empty at the output ceiling, while
\texttt{Claude Sonnet 5} and \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini} stay within $\pm 0.05$, though all three state
more milestones.

\paragraph{Open Tool.} An offered interpreter does not move the closed models: neither
\texttt{Claude Sonnet 5} nor \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini} changes in FAC or MC beyond $\pm 0.05$,
although \texttt{Claude Sonnet 5} calls it on two thirds of the instances, a bound that says little
for a model already near the ceiling.
The interpreter is offered rather than required, and only to the two closed models, since no
admitted endpoint serves the open-weights model's tool calls.

Most wrong answers are calculation slips, and conceptual errors grow with difficulty.
Three domain experts read the full response of 137 wrong answers from four models that span the
FAC order, all at the providers' default settings, and assigned the first category that applies in a
six-category hierarchy from hallucination to calculation, or ``no error'' (Fleiss'~$\kappa$
0.912~\citep{fleiss1971}; \autoref{fig:error_categories}).
Calculation is the majority label for \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini}, \texttt{Gemma 4 26B}, and
\texttt{Claude Sonnet 5} and the largest for \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, the model with the most
conceptual errors: a hallucination, a wrong setup, or a wrong formula.
Such conceptual errors are rarer on Easy problems than on Intermediate and Advanced ones
(\autoref{tab:error_by_level}).
The reading agrees with the reasoning setting and the experiments, each agreement resting on one
or two models: \texttt{GPT-5.4 mini} and \texttt{Gemma 4 26B}, whose wrong answers are mostly
calculation slips, gain the most from reasoning, and the equations lift only \texttt{gpt-oss-20b},
the model with the most conceptual errors.

The first five lose few answer points, and the largest share goes to incorrect verdicts.
Over 11,250 responses, the first five models lose 231 answer points.
Of these, 71 go to responses with no readable final answer and 34 to 68 partial answers at half
credit.
Of their 126 incorrect verdicts, 38 lie within 0.2\% of a target whose digits the question
prescribes, which makes them incorrect by its terms, 5 are symbolic answers scored by the numbers
they state, and 83 fall in neither group (\autoref{appendix:error_analysis}).
On the final answer, then, the evaluation set is near ceiling for the first five, and most of what
still differs among them lies in the derivation: how closely it follows the gold route, and how
often its displayed arithmetic slips, which the arithmetic check flags in 0.6\% to 14.3\% of their
correct answers, at rates that depend on how much arithmetic each displays.
Since a response that misstates the rule it applies but computes the right answer passes every
deterministic check, the process measures mark where to read, and only a reader can confirm such a
rule.
