\section{Validation Against Expert Judgment}
\label{appendix:validation}

This section details the validation of~\autoref{subsec:validation}: the expert study, the evaluator
designs compared on it, the planted defects, judge independence, and the domain experts' readings
of the evaluated models' responses.

\subsection{Expert Study}

\paragraph{Responses.}
The study draws four instances from each of 15 templates, 60 in all, across the five branches and
three levels.
Only 12 of the 60 instances (20\%) have a single scalar answer, against 89 of the 150 templates, so
the study is a hard test of answer checking.
Five LLMs outside the evaluated models, \texttt{GPT-5}, \texttt{Claude Opus 4.7},
\texttt{Gemini 3.1 Pro}, \texttt{DeepSeek-R1}, and \texttt{Llama 3.1 70B}, answer every instance
once, giving 300 responses.

\paragraph{Labels.}
Fifteen domain experts, three per branch, label every response of their branch, behind opaque codes
and with assignments fixed in advance.
Each expert labels every step as correct, alternative correct, incorrect (with an error type and a
written reason), or not a claim; the status of every milestone; the final answer as correct,
partial, incorrect, or not stated; and whether the reasoning is sound.
Three rounds follow the first pass: every one of the 1,042 steps labeled incorrect receives a
reason; each expert labels seven of their own responses again, blind to their first pass; and every
step on which a branch's experts split two to one, 272 steps in 134 responses, returns to all three
experts, blind and with the three labels anonymized and shuffled, and the majority of this second
pass becomes the label.

\paragraph{Agreement.}
Between experts, Fleiss'~$\kappa$~\citep{fleiss1971} is 0.781 on steps (from 0.645 in civil to
0.867 in industrial engineering), 0.880 on milestones, and 0.966 on final answers; within an
expert, Cohen's~$\kappa$~\citep{cohen1960} between the two passes is 0.828.
Adjudication changes 85 step labels and raises the number of steps labeled incorrect from 322 to
388.

\paragraph{Statistical Power.}
The 300 responses come from 15 templates, so every interval resamples templates; the design effect
of 2.6 to 4.2 makes the study worth about 70 to 115 independent responses.
For evaluators built differently from the step-matching design below, the smallest difference from
it that the study detects in the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) at the response level is 0.12 to
0.19, so a null result here means no difference of that size.

\subsection{Evaluator Designs Compared}

We compare seven designs on these labels: a step-matching design, which aligns the response's steps
with the gold steps by semantic similarity and numerical agreement and sends the steps it cannot
align to a panel of LLM judges from evaluated families (\texttt{GPT-5}, \texttt{Claude Opus 4.5},
and \texttt{Gemini 3.1 Pro}), with two and with all three judges voting; the same design with a
panel from outside the evaluated families (\texttt{Grok 4.6}, \texttt{MiniMax M3}, and
\texttt{MiMo-V2.5-Pro}); three process reward models (\texttt{Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B} and
\texttt{Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B}~\citep{zhang2025prmlessons}, and
\texttt{VersaPRM}~\citep{zeng2025versaprm}); milestone matching; matching with the arithmetic
check; and matching with the judge.
\autoref{tab:evaluator_comparison} gives the agreement of each component with the experts: the
final-answer check and milestone matching with the judge agree closely with them, whereas the
step-level checks find at most 0.603 of the steps that the experts mark incorrect, which is why we
report these checks as flags with their precision.

Whether the answer is right nearly decides the response-level verdict: the experts' own
final-answer verdict predicts their verdict on a response's soundness at AUROC 0.974, and they call
only 3 of the 228 correct-answer responses unsound.
Evaluators therefore have to be compared at the level of steps and milestones.
The experts mark at least one incorrect step in 93 of the 228 correct-answer responses, 178 steps
in all, and 175 of the 178 are calculation slips; such a slip rarely changes milestone coverage,
and finding it is the task of the step-level diagnostics.
The best reward model, \texttt{Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B}, ranks steps well (AUROC 0.825), but its
precision is 0.539 over all steps and 0.246 inside correct-answer responses, and fitting its
threshold on one half of the responses changes its F1 on the other half by $-0.006$.
A panel from outside the evaluated families moves the step-matching design's mean score from 0.379
to 0.378.

\subsection{Planted Defects}

From the 129 responses that the experts label clean and the final-answer check scores correct, we
plant exactly one defect in each of 120 of them and keep 60 untouched as controls.
An arithmetic defect changes one digit of one displayed intermediate value and leaves the later
steps and the final answer as they were; a conceptual defect changes the reasoning a step states,
by flipping a criterion, misstating a rule, or stating a formula that contradicts the one used, and
leaves every digit of the response unchanged.
We verify every defect without the checker under test: an arithmetic defect by recomputing its
calculation at full precision or against the template's value, and a conceptual defect by
confirming that the response's digits are byte-identical to the original.
Every deterministic check detects 0 of the 60 conceptual defects.

We ask four LLM judges about each defect with the step prompt of~\autoref{appendix:scoring} and
one step under review, once in the planted response and once in the untouched original, and count
a defect as caught only when the judge flags the planted step and not the original
(\autoref{tab:planted_detection}).
\texttt{GPT-5} (OpenAI) and \texttt{Claude Opus 4.5} (Anthropic) share families with evaluated
models, whereas \texttt{MiMo-V2.5-Pro} (Xiaomi) and \texttt{Grok 4.6} (xAI) do not; no judge catches
more than 22 of the 60 conceptual defects.
In the step-matching design, a conceptual defect reaches a judge exactly as often as the same step
untouched (0.500), and the design catches 0.150 of them end to end; the judged step check sends 58
of the 60 to the judge and catches 19.

\subsection{Judge Independence}

We choose \texttt{MiMo-V2.5-Pro} because it shares no family with an evaluated model and catches
about as many planted defects as \texttt{GPT-5} (\autoref{tab:planted_detection}).
To test the single-judge design, \texttt{Grok 4.6}, from a third family, re-judges with the same
prompts the milestones that the judge decided in 20 responses per model, drawn from 14 to 19
templates (\autoref{tab:judge_swap}).
Each model's coverage of these responses changes by $-0.017$ to $+0.020$, and every 95\% interval,
which resamples templates, includes zero; the sample checks each model's coverage, not the
differences between models.
On the step-matching design's panel, replayed offline on the expert study, dropping the judge from
a response model's own family changes that model's score by at most 0.006, as much as dropping
another family's judge, yet each judge calls 65\% to 70\% of the steps it judges that the experts
mark incorrect alternative correct.
An absence of self-preference therefore does not show that a judge is accurate.

\subsection{Readings on the Evaluated Models}

\paragraph{Arithmetic Flags.}
A domain expert reads a sample of the arithmetic check's flags on the evaluated models' responses:
up to 20 per model, spread over each model's templates, shown without the model's name, and none
read while the check was developed.
The expert labels each flag a real slip, a checker error, or unsure; of 190 flags, 171 are slips,
18 are checker errors, and 1 is unsure, so 171 of the 189 decided flags are real, a precision of
0.905 (Wilson 95\% interval~\citep{wilson1927} 0.854 to 0.939; 0.632 to 1.000 per model).

\paragraph{Verdicts and Rulings.}
Fifteen domain experts, two per item from the item's branch, read 150 final-answer verdicts on the
five strongest models, sampled by the check's verdict (75 correct, 49 incorrect, and 26 partial),
and 100 of the judge's rulings across all eleven models (50 reached and 50 missing).
\autoref{tab:expert_readings} gives their readings by verdict; the two readers agree on 97\% of the
final answers (Cohen's~$\kappa$ 0.947) and 88\% of the milestones ($\kappa$ 0.803).
The experts call most partial verdicts fully correct (34 of 52 readings), so scoring a partial
answer 0.5 is conservative.
Of the judge's missing rulings, the experts find in 33 of 100 readings that the response's route
does not need the milestone; the strict reading counts these as missing, so MC is lower for a
response that takes another route.
