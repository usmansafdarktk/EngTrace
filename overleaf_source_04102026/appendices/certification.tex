\section{Template Certification}
\label{appendix:certification}

This section details the three checks of~\autoref{subsec:certification}, the automated integrity
checks, the LLM screen, and the expert certification, and reports their results.

\subsection{Automated Integrity Checks}

We run every template at 500 seeds, and every seed must pass five checks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Closure}: every printed calculation, recomputed from its printed operands, gives
    its printed result at the printed precision.
    Lines the check cannot parse, such as a ratio rewritten across units or a result rounded in a
    direction that the line states, are recorded with the reason and checked by hand: six line
    patterns in four templates.
    \item \textbf{Determinism}: the same seed regenerates the same instance in a separate process.
    \item \textbf{Output format}: every solution has step markers numbered from one without gaps,
    exactly one answer marker, and a non-empty question and solution.
    \item \textbf{Clean output}: no printed expression is malformed, such as a doubled sign or a
    zero coefficient printed as a product.
    \item \textbf{Generation}: every seed produces an instance without error.
\end{itemize}
All 150 templates pass, and we edited 54 templates to make them pass.

\subsection{LLM Screen}

Three LLM judges score every template: \texttt{Grok 4.6} (xAI), \texttt{MiniMax M3} (MiniMax), and
\texttt{MiMo-V2.5-Pro} (Xiaomi), none of them from the family of an evaluated model.
We query them through OpenRouter at temperature 0, with the output constrained to JSON.
Each judge receives the template's source code and three of its instances in the prompt below and
scores them on the rubric of~\autoref{tab:qa_rubric}.
A template is a critical failure when at least two judges flag it for human review or when the
median score of any dimension is below 4; otherwise, it is controversial when the population
standard deviation of the three judges' scores exceeds 0.5 on some dimension, and it passes when
neither holds.

In the first pass, 126 templates pass, 13 are controversial, and 11 are critical failures; the
judges agree on the review flag with Gwet's AC1 of 0.84~\citep{gwet2008}.
Before any edit, we check each claim in the judges' explanations against the code and the
instances; 34 of the 45 claims hold, and we revise the 24 flagged templates.
In the second pass, which re-judges the revised templates, 147 templates pass, 3 are controversial,
and none is a critical failure (AC1 of 0.93).
Against the experts' first-round verdicts, the screen is a weak filter: 15 of the 147 templates it
passes are rejected by a majority of their experts, a false-positive rate of 10.2\%.
The mean absolute difference between the median scores of the experts and of the judges is 0.587
for physical plausibility, 0.187 for mathematical correctness, and 0.787 for pedagogical clarity.

\subsection{Expert Certification}

Fifteen domain experts certify the templates, three per branch, each reviewing only their own
branch.
Each expert receives 34 items, the 30 templates of the branch and four planted defects, shuffled in
an order of their own under opaque item codes.
Experts are told that quality-control items exist but not which items they are, they do not see the
screen's verdicts, and the three experts of a branch do not discuss a template until all three have
submitted.
For each item, an expert solves one instance by hand and enters the answer before the solution is
shown, and the interface reports whether the answer matches the gold value within 1\%.
The expert then reads the worked solution and, if needed, the code, cycles through five instances,
scores physical plausibility, mathematical correctness, and pedagogical clarity from 1 to 5, and
approves or rejects the template; a rejection requires a written reason and a defect type.
The interface records the time spent on each item.
Each planted defect is a real template of the branch with one error written into it
(\autoref{tab:planted_defects}); a wrong constant, unit conversion, or sign is carried consistently
through the computation, the printed steps, and the answer, whereas a wrong printed result changes a
single printed line.

\subsection{Results}

Every expert rejects all four planted defects of their branch, 60 of 60 readings.
Of the 504 first-round hand checks with a comparable number, 459 match the gold value within 1\%,
and 44 of the 45 mismatches end in a rejection.
\autoref{tab:expert_agreement} gives the agreement among the three experts of each branch in the
first round: Fleiss' $\kappa$ on approval falls where nearly every template is
approved~\citep{feinstein1990high}, whereas Gwet's AC1 and its weighted form AC2, used for the 1--5
scores, do not.

In the first round, at least one expert rejects 22 templates, 15 of them by a majority: chemical 9,
electrical 3, and mechanical 10, while every civil and industrial expert approves all 30 templates
of their branch (\autoref{tab:certification_rounds}).
We check each rejection against the instances the expert saw; we revise 20 of them and, for the
other 2, record in the template why we keep it unchanged, and all 22 return to the same experts.
In the second round, 17 are approved by all three experts, 2 by a majority, and 3 are rejected by a
majority; we revise the five templates with a rejection, and the third round approves all five
unanimously.
A fourth round re-certifies two chemical templates that we extended so that each yields 15 distinct
questions.
Every template is thus approved unanimously in the latest round that reviewed it, 126 in the first
round, 17 in the second, 5 in the third, and 2 in the fourth, and its current code regenerates, byte
for byte, the instances its experts reviewed.
