\section{Template Certification}
\label{appendix:certification}

This section details the three checks of~\autoref{subsec:certification} and their results.

\paragraph{Automated Integrity Checks.} We run every template at 500 seeds, and every seed must
generate an instance without error, regenerate it identically in a separate process, keep the
output format (numbered steps and one answer marker), print no malformed expression, and pass the
closure check: every printed calculation, recomputed from its printed operands, gives its printed
result at the printed precision.
Lines the closure check cannot parse, six line patterns in four templates, are checked by hand, and
all 150 templates pass.

\paragraph{LLM Screen.} Three LLM judges from families outside the evaluated models,
\texttt{Grok 4.6}~\citep{grok46}, \texttt{MiniMax M3}~\citep{minimaxm3}, and
\texttt{MiMo-V2.5-Pro}, score each template's code and three of its instances with the prompt
below, at temperature 0.
A template fails critically when at least two judges flag it for review or the median score of any
dimension is below 4, is controversial when the judges' scores on some dimension have a population
standard deviation above 0.5, and passes otherwise.
In the first pass, 126 templates pass, 13 are controversial, and 11 are critical failures (Gwet's
AC1 of 0.84 on the flag~\citep{gwet2008}); 34 of the 45 claims in the judges' explanations hold
against the code and the instances, and after we revise the 24 flagged templates, 147 templates
pass, 3 are controversial, and none is a critical failure (AC1 of 0.93).
Against the experts' first-round decisions, 15 of the 147 templates the screen passes are rejected
by a majority of their experts, a false-positive rate of 10.2\%, and the median scores of experts
and judges differ on average by 0.587 for physical plausibility, 0.187 for mathematical
correctness, and 0.787 for pedagogical clarity.

\paragraph{Expert Certification.} Three domain experts per branch each review the 30 templates of
their branch mixed with four planted defects, in an order of their own under opaque codes, knowing
that planted items exist but not which; they do not see the screen's results or discuss a template
before all three have submitted.
For each item, an expert solves one instance by hand before the solution is shown, and the
interface reports whether the answer is within 1\% of the gold value; the expert then reads the
solution and, as needed, the code and the template's other four instances, scores the three
dimensions from 1 to 5, and approves or rejects, naming a defect type and a reason for a rejection.
Each planted defect is a real template of the branch with one error written into it
(\autoref{tab:planted_defects}); a wrong constant, unit conversion, or sign is carried through the
computation, the printed steps, and the answer, whereas a wrong printed result changes a single
printed line.

\paragraph{Results.} Every expert rejects all four planted defects of their branch, 60 of 60
readings.
Of the 510 first-round hand checks, 504 have a number to compare; 459 of these match the gold value
within 1\%, and 44 of the 45 mismatches end in a rejection.
The planted defects test whether an expert catches a wrong number, through a constant, a unit
conversion, a sign, or a printed result; they do not test whether a question states everything its
answer depends on, which the domain experts' reading of six near-miss templates examines
(\autoref{appendix:error_analysis}).
\autoref{tab:expert_agreement} gives the first-round agreement within each branch.
Fleiss' $\kappa$ is undefined for civil and industrial, where every decision is an approval, and is
deflated where nearly every template is approved~\citep{feinstein1990high}: in electrical, 5 of the
90 decisions on templates are rejections, so 90\% agreement gives a $\kappa$ of 0.365.
Gwet's AC1 and AC2 are not deflated in this way.
In the first round, 43 of the 450 decisions on templates are rejections, and at least one expert
rejects 22 templates (chemical 9, electrical 3, and mechanical 10), 15 of them by a majority, while
every civil and industrial expert approves all 30 templates of their branch.

A template that any expert rejects, or whose code changes, returns to the same three experts,
revised where the rejection holds and with a hand-check instance none of them has seen, until all
three approve it; certification takes six rounds:
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
\item in the second round, 10 of the 66 decisions are rejections, on 5 of the 22 templates;
\item the third round approves all 5, and its two hand checks that do not match give the gold value
in pascals where the gold answer is in kilopascals;
\item a fourth round re-certifies two templates whose sampled parameters are widened so that each
supplies 15 distinct instances;
\item a fifth round re-certifies two templates whose questions state the system, the form of the
equation, and the property data on which the answer depends, and one of its 6 decisions is a
rejection, on the physical plausibility of compressing organic compounds above their decomposition
temperatures;
\item a sixth round re-certifies one template, with organic compounds held below their decomposition
temperatures, and all three experts approve it.
\end{itemize}
In rounds 2 to 6, 66 of 66, 13 of 15, 6 of 6, 6 of 6, and 3 of 3 hand checks match the gold value.
Every template is thus approved unanimously in the latest round that reviewed it, and its current
code regenerates, byte for byte, the instances its experts reviewed.
